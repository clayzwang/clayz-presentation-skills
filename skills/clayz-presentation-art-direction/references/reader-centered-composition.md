# Reader-centered composition

Layout organizes attention and understanding. It helps a reader perceive what
matters, which information belongs together, how a relationship works and where
to read next. Comfortable pacing supports sustained attention. Alignment and
legibility provide a foundation; the composition must also do explanatory work.

Use these prompts while designing and reviewing actual pages. They are core Art
guidance, not a learning package, template collection or machine-scored gate.
Art owns the judgments and revisions within the existing content boundaries.

## Start with the reader's task

Read the whole story and identify what this page helps the reader understand.
Find the main judgment, its evidence, the relationships and the necessary
conditions. Decide what should become apparent before the reader works through
every paragraph. The first visual may be a contrast, relationship, trend or
critical condition; choosing the title as the first visual on every page does
not complete this decision.

A textual heading marks meaning. It does not require a separate box, equal
area or the same visual weight as another heading. Choose grouping and emphasis
according to the communication task, while preserving the approved meaning.
If the text cannot support a useful visual relationship, return the specific
ambiguity or required wording change to Copy instead of inventing one.

## Let space explain relationships

Proximity, separation, alignment, nesting, scale and connections can make a
relationship perceptible. Select the combination that suits this content.
The placement should help readers recognize grouping, comparison, sequence,
conditions or feedback with less reconstruction from prose. An arrangement
must not invent a sequence, cause, rank or numerical proportion.

Distinguish different relationships within a page. A process may have a main
path, conditions attached to particular actions and an accompanying explanation
of revenue. They need not receive the same spatial treatment. Boxes and arrows
are available tools, with meaning determined by their actual use. Avoid choosing
a grid from the number of headings and then finding reasons to justify it.

Several related numbers can share a comparison or other visual encoding when
the approved data supports it. Labels, units and conditions belong close enough
to the evidence to be understood together. A chart that merely repeats all the
prose adds work; request a precise Copy revision when the selected medium needs
different wording. Tabular presentations retain the native-table requirements.

## Give attention a useful hierarchy

Use size, position, contrast, spacing, area and alignment together to guide the
reader. Main evidence should receive the attention needed to understand the
claim; necessary conditions remain readable and attached to what they qualify.
Equal editorial levels may have different visual weights when their roles differ.

Look at what actually attracts the eye. A border, icon or empty container can
dominate even when its text is minor. Reallocate the space or emphasis when the
visible hierarchy misrepresents the information. Whitespace can separate ideas,
support a focal point or give the eye a pause. Judge those effects on the actual
page, including whether unused space could relieve cramped information.

## Compose a sequence the reader can sustain

Inspect the actual sequence as well as each page. Repeated placement can help
comparison and orientation. Check that the comparison dimensions stay coherent,
the new evidence receives attention and the reader benefits from the shared
structure. A claim that repetition "supports comparison" needs to be visible
in those pages.

Visual fatigue can arise when evidence, explanation, process and conclusion all
require the same scanning effort and receive the same emphasis. Consider the
combined effect of silhouette, density, focal point, medium and reading path.
Each page can have a plausible local explanation while the sequence still feels
monotonous. Art must notice and revise that experience.

Retain the story and useful continuity. Change composition, emphasis, grouping
or pacing where they improve understanding. A different color, icon or random
layout rotation does not establish a different reading experience. There is no
fixed repetition limit, prescribed number of layouts or required contrast page.

Shared drawing code may handle text measurement and native objects. Its default
coordinates, column widths or container count must not become the design brief.
Decide the composition first and implement the chosen design with suitable tools.

## Inspect, revise and observe the result

Use thumbnails to judge the first impression, focal points and the sequence.
Read full-size pages to judge relationships, conditions, density and reading
comfort. Compare the intended experience with the actual experience: what is
noticed first, what remains hard to connect, and where the eye or reader tires.
Seeing all the text or staying inside containers proves only part of the work.

When a problem is visible, name its concrete effect and revise the design that
causes it. Then inspect the changed page and its place in the sequence again.
Use existing work notes to record material observations, actual changes and
their observed effects. Retained issues need specific reasons; stock statements
about tidy boxes or comparable sections do not demonstrate improvement.

Art performs this judgment before locking the baseline. Output implements it;
audit does not supply missing design decisions. Preserve the existing handoff,
native editing boundaries and responsibility for wording changes. Do not add
an aesthetic score, extra approval form or automatic layout quota.

## Illustrative ways to think

These hypothetical page tasks illustrate reasoning, not reusable layouts:

- A payment undertaking: help readers see the participants, movement of documents
  and payment conditions. Place an accompanying fee explanation where its role
  is clear, without turning it into an invented final process step.
- A safety review: make the supported relationship among incoming reports,
  evaluation and possible changes to use conditions apparent. Preserve the
  distinction between a reported event and evidence of causation.
- A performance comparison: make a supported difference visible alongside its
  period, unit and scope. Let the reader inspect the evidence behind the claim
  without assembling it from unrelated paragraph boxes.

Choose an original composition for the actual content. The measure of these
ideas is whether the reader can see and understand that content more readily.
